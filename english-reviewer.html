📖 GRADE 9 ENGLISH

FIRST TO FOURTH QUARTER

Grammar • Literature • Listening • Critical Reading

FIRST QUARTER — GRAMMAR AND COMMUNICATION

1. Modals of Permission, Obligation, and Prohibition

What are Modals?

Modals are helping verbs used with the main verb to express ability, possibility, permission, advice, obligation, or prohibition.

Common Modals

  • Can: Ability or informal permission.
  • Could: Past ability or polite request.
  • May: Permission or possibility.
  • Might: Possibility.
  • Must: Strong obligation or necessity.
  • Should: Advice or recommendation.
  • Must not: Prohibition.
You must submit your project on time. (Obligation)
You may leave the room. (Permission)
You must not cheat during the exam. (Prohibition)
REMEMBER: Modals are followed by the base form of the main verb.
2. Conditional Sentences

What are Conditional Sentences?

Conditional sentences express a condition and its possible result. They commonly use "if."

Four Types

  • Zero Conditional: General truths. If + present simple, present simple.
  • First Conditional: Real future possibility. If + present simple, will + base verb.
  • Second Conditional: Imaginary or unlikely present/future situation. If + past simple, would + base verb.
  • Third Conditional: Imaginary past situation. If + past perfect, would have + past participle.
Zero: If you heat ice, it melts.
First: If I study, I will pass.
Second: If I had wings, I would fly.
Third: If I had studied, I would have passed.
REMEMBER: Zero = fact; First = possible future; Second = unreal present/future; Third = unreal past.
3. Word Formation

What is Word Formation?

Word formation is the process of creating new words from existing words or word parts.

  • Clipping: Shortening a word. Example: advertisement → ad.
  • Blending: Combining parts of two words. Example: breakfast + lunch → brunch.
  • Acronym: A word formed from initial letters and pronounced as a word. Example: NASA.
  • Compounding: Combining two complete words. Example: toothbrush.
  • Affixation: Adding a prefix or suffix. Example: happy → unhappy.
REMEMBER: Clipping shortens; blending combines parts; compounding joins complete words.
4. Context Clues

What are Context Clues?

Context clues are hints in a sentence or paragraph that help readers understand unfamiliar words.

Types of Context Clues

  • Definition: The meaning is directly explained.
  • Synonym: A similar word gives a hint.
  • Antonym: An opposite word provides a clue.
  • Example: Examples help explain the word.
  • Inference: Readers use surrounding information to figure out the meaning.
"The room was enormous, meaning it was very large."
The phrase "very large" explains enormous.
REMEMBER: Read the words around an unfamiliar word before using a dictionary.
5. Communicative Styles

What are Communicative Styles?

Communicative styles are ways of speaking depending on the situation, audience, and relationship between speakers.

  • Intimate: Used with people who share a close relationship.
  • Casual: Informal conversations with friends or familiar people.
  • Consultative: Polite, two-way communication, often in professional situations.
  • Formal: Used in serious or official situations.
  • Frozen: Fixed expressions that rarely change, such as traditional ceremonies or pledges.
Casual: "Hey! How are you?"
Formal: "Good morning. May I ask a question?"
Frozen: A traditional oath or pledge.
REMEMBER: Choose your speaking style based on your audience and situation.

SECOND QUARTER — READING AND LITERATURE

6. Comparing and Contrasting Texts

What is Comparing and Contrasting?

Comparing identifies similarities between two or more texts. Contrasting identifies their differences.

Common Signal Words

  • Similarities: Likewise, similarly, both, also.
  • Differences: However, unlike, whereas, on the other hand.
Both stories have brave main characters.
However, one takes place in the past while the other happens in modern times.
REMEMBER: Compare = similarities. Contrast = differences.
7. Connecting Texts to Real-Life Issues

What is Text-to-World Connection?

It means relating the ideas or events in a text to actual situations, experiences, or issues in the world.

Types of Connections

  • Text-to-Self: Connecting a text to your personal experiences.
  • Text-to-Text: Connecting one text to another text.
  • Text-to-World: Connecting a text to real-world events and issues.
A story about bullying can be connected to the real-life problem of bullying in schools.
REMEMBER: Connections help readers understand why a text matters in real life.
8. Culture, History, and Environment in Literature

How Do These Influence Literature?

Literary works are influenced by the time, place, culture, and experiences of the people who create them.

  • Culture: Traditions, beliefs, values, and customs.
  • History: Important events and experiences from the past.
  • Environment: The natural and social surroundings of people.
A story written during a war may show fear, loss, courage, and the effects of conflict on families.
REMEMBER: Understanding context helps explain the meaning of a literary work.
9. Literature and Human Values

What are Human Values?

Human values are principles that guide people's actions and decisions.

Examples of Values in Literature

  • Honesty — telling the truth.
  • Respect — treating others properly.
  • Compassion — showing concern for others.
  • Responsibility — accepting duties and consequences.
  • Perseverance — continuing despite difficulties.
  • Justice — treating people fairly.
A character who returns a lost wallet demonstrates honesty.
REMEMBER: Literature can teach lessons about human behavior and values.
10. Literature in a VUCA World

What does VUCA mean?

VUCA describes a world that changes quickly and can be difficult to understand or predict.

  • Volatility: Rapid and unpredictable changes.
  • Uncertainty: Difficulty knowing what will happen.
  • Complexity: Many connected factors make situations difficult.
  • Ambiguity: Lack of clear meaning or understanding.

How Does Literature Help?

  • Develops empathy and understanding.
  • Encourages critical thinking.
  • Shows different perspectives.
  • Helps readers reflect on real-life challenges.
REMEMBER: VUCA = Volatility, Uncertainty, Complexity, Ambiguity.

THIRD QUARTER — LISTENING AND VIEWING

11. Bias vs. Prejudice

What is Bias?

Bias is a preference or inclination that may affect a person's judgment.

What is Prejudice?

Prejudice is a preconceived judgment or opinion about a person or group without sufficient knowledge or evidence.

  • Bias: A tendency to favor one side or perspective.
  • Prejudice: An unfair judgment formed before knowing the facts.
Bias: A reviewer always favors one brand.
Prejudice: Assuming someone is incapable based only on their background.
REMEMBER: Both can affect fair and objective judgment.
12. Evaluating Viewed Information

What Should You Check?

When watching videos, news reports, or presentations, evaluate whether the information is relevant, accurate, and trustworthy.

  • Relevance: Is the information related to the topic?
  • Accuracy: Is the information correct?
  • Credibility: Is the source reliable?
  • Currency: Is the information up to date?
  • Purpose: Is the material meant to inform, persuade, or entertain?
Before sharing a news video, check its source, publication date, and supporting evidence.
REMEMBER: Do not believe or share information without checking its source.
13. Evaluating Evidence from Listening Materials

What is Evidence?

Evidence is information used to support a statement, claim, or argument.

Types of Evidence

  • Facts: Statements that can be checked or verified.
  • Statistics: Numerical information or data.
  • Examples: Specific situations that support an idea.
  • Expert Testimony: Information from someone with relevant knowledge or experience.
A speaker claims that reading improves vocabulary and supports the claim using research findings.
REMEMBER: Strong arguments use relevant and reliable evidence.
14. Understanding the Speaker's Purpose

What is the Speaker's Purpose?

It is the reason why a person delivers a speech or communicates a message.

  • To Inform: To provide facts or explain something.
  • To Persuade: To influence opinions or actions.
  • To Entertain: To amuse or interest the audience.
  • To Inspire: To encourage or motivate people.
A speech explaining climate change aims to inform.
A campaign speech asking people to support a cause aims to persuade.
REMEMBER: Ask yourself, "What does the speaker want the audience to think, feel, or do?"
15. Analyzing Information from Different Media

What is Media Analysis?

Media analysis means examining how information is presented through television, radio, websites, newspapers, and social media.

What to Examine

  • Source and author.
  • Purpose and intended audience.
  • Facts, opinions, and supporting evidence.
  • Language, images, and presentation.
  • Possible bias or misleading information.
Two news reports may describe the same event differently. Compare their sources and evidence before forming a conclusion.
REMEMBER: Think critically before accepting information from any media source.

FOURTH QUARTER — CRITICAL READING AND ANALYSIS

16. Evaluating the Relevance and Worth of Ideas

What is Relevance?

Relevance refers to how closely information relates to the topic or question.

What is Worth?

Worth refers to the usefulness, importance, or value of an idea or information.

  • Check whether the idea answers the question.
  • Determine whether it is supported by evidence.
  • Consider whether it is useful and important.
REMEMBER: Relevant information is connected to the topic; valuable information is useful and meaningful.
17. Evaluating an Author's Reasoning

What is Reasoning?

Reasoning is the process of using facts, evidence, and logical thinking to support a conclusion.

Important Parts of an Argument

  • Claim: The main point or position.
  • Evidence: Information supporting the claim.
  • Reasoning: Explains how the evidence supports the claim.
  • Conclusion: The final idea based on the argument.
Claim: Students should read every day.
Evidence: Reading helps develop vocabulary.
Reasoning: Regular reading exposes students to new words and their meanings.
REMEMBER: A strong argument has a clear claim, relevant evidence, and logical reasoning.
18. Facts and Opinions

What is a Fact?

A fact is a statement that can be verified or proven true or false.

What is an Opinion?

An opinion expresses a person's belief, feeling, or judgment.

Fact: The Philippines is an archipelago.
Opinion: The Philippines is the most beautiful country.

Clues for Opinions

  • I think...
  • In my opinion...
  • Best, worst, beautiful, boring, wonderful.
REMEMBER: Facts can be verified. Opinions express personal views.
19. Making Value Judgments

What is a Value Judgment?

A value judgment is an evaluation of something based on standards, principles, or beliefs about what is good, bad, fair, or important.

How to Make a Good Judgment

  • Understand the issue.
  • Consider different perspectives.
  • Use reliable information.
  • Apply fair and reasonable standards.
  • Explain your decision.
A student judges a school policy by considering whether it is fair, helpful, and supported by good reasons.
REMEMBER: A strong value judgment is fair, thoughtful, and supported by reasons.
20. Formulating Evidence-Based Opinions

What is an Evidence-Based Opinion?

It is a personal position supported by reliable facts, examples, or research.

Steps

  1. Understand the issue.
  2. Gather reliable information.
  3. Consider different viewpoints.
  4. Form your position.
  5. Support your opinion with evidence.
Opinion: Schools should encourage reading.
Evidence: Regular reading helps learners develop vocabulary and comprehension.
REMEMBER: Opinion + Reliable Evidence + Clear Explanation = Stronger Argument.

FIRST TERM — LITERATURE AND TEXT ANALYSIS

1. World Literature

What is World Literature?

World Literature refers to literary works from different countries and cultures.

Examples of Literary Works

  • Poetry: Uses rhythm, imagery, and figurative language.
  • Short Story: A brief fictional narrative.
  • Novel: A long fictional story.
  • Drama: A literary work written to be performed.
  • Myth: A traditional story that may explain beliefs or natural events.
  • Folktale: A traditional story passed down through generations.
Reading stories from different countries helps us understand different cultures and perspectives.
REMEMBER: World Literature = Literary works from different countries and cultures.
2. Sociocultural Context

What is Sociocultural Context?

It refers to the social and cultural conditions surrounding a literary work.

  • Culture: Beliefs, customs, and traditions.
  • Society: A community of people.
  • History: Events from the past.
  • Religion: Beliefs and practices related to faith.
  • Social Class: People's position in society.
  • Environment: The surroundings that influence people's lives.
A story about arranged marriage may reflect the traditions and expectations of a particular society.
REMEMBER: Sociocultural Context = Society and culture surrounding a literary work.
3. Character and Characterization

What is a Character?

A character is a person, animal, or being that takes part in a story.

Types of Characters

  • Protagonist: The central character.
  • Antagonist: The character or force opposing the protagonist.
  • Major Character: Plays an important role.
  • Minor Character: Has a smaller role.
  • Dynamic Character: Undergoes an important change.
  • Static Character: Remains mostly the same.

Methods of Characterization

  • Direct: The author directly tells the reader about the character.
  • Indirect: The reader discovers traits through actions, dialogue, thoughts, and interactions.
Direct: "Maria was kind."
Indirect: "Maria shared her lunch with a classmate."
REMEMBER: Character = Who is in the story. Characterization = How personality is revealed.
4. Literary Conflict

What is Conflict?

Conflict is the struggle or problem experienced by a character.

  • Internal Conflict: A struggle within a character's mind or emotions.
  • External Conflict: A struggle between a character and an outside force.
  • Character vs. Self: A struggle with personal decisions or feelings.
  • Character vs. Character: A struggle between people.
  • Character vs. Society: A struggle against social rules or expectations.
  • Character vs. Nature: A struggle against natural forces.
  • Character vs. Technology: A struggle involving machines or technology.
  • Character vs. Supernatural: A struggle against mysterious forces.
REMEMBER: Conflict = The problem or struggle in a story.
5. Plot Structure

What is Plot?

Plot is the sequence of events in a story.

Five Parts of Plot

  1. Exposition: Introduces characters, setting, and situation.
  2. Rising Action: Develops the conflict and builds tension.
  3. Climax: The most intense turning point.
  4. Falling Action: Events after the climax.
  5. Resolution: The ending or conclusion.

Types of Plot Structure

  • Linear: Events happen in chronological order.
  • Flashback: The story goes back to an earlier event.
  • Episodic: The story is divided into connected episodes.
  • Parallel: Two or more storylines develop alongside one another.
REMEMBER: Exposition → Rising Action → Climax → Falling Action → Resolution.
6. Point of View

What is Point of View?

Point of view is the perspective from which a story is told.

  • First Person: Uses I, me, my, or we.
  • Second Person: Uses you or your.
  • Third Person Limited: Follows the thoughts and feelings of one character.
  • Third Person Omniscient: Knows the thoughts and feelings of multiple characters.
  • Third Person Objective: Reports actions and dialogue without revealing inner thoughts.
First: "I walked into the classroom."
Second: "You walk into the classroom."
Third: "She walked into the classroom."
REMEMBER: First = I/We; Second = You; Third = He/She/They.
7. Narrative Techniques

What are Narrative Techniques?

These are methods writers use to tell a story and make it more interesting.

  • Flashback: Takes the reader back to an earlier event.
  • Foreshadowing: Gives hints about what may happen later.
  • Suspense: Creates excitement or uncertainty.
  • Imagery: Uses descriptive language that appeals to the senses.
  • Symbolism: Uses something to represent a deeper meaning.
  • Dialogue: Conversation between characters.
Dark clouds gathering before a storm may be an example of foreshadowing.
REMEMBER: Flashback = Past event. Foreshadowing = Hint about the future.
8. Literary Analysis

What is Literary Analysis?

It is the careful examination of a literary work to understand its meaning, message, and techniques.

  • Theme: The central message or insight about life.
  • Setting: The time and place of the story.
  • Character: The people or beings in the story.
  • Plot: The sequence of events.
  • Conflict: The struggle in the story.
  • Tone: The author's attitude toward the subject.
  • Mood: The feeling created in the reader.
  • Symbolism: Objects or events representing deeper meanings.

Theme vs. Main Idea

Theme is a message about life. Main idea is what a passage is mostly about.

REMEMBER: Tone = Author's attitude. Mood = Reader's feeling.
9. Drama and Prose

What is Drama?

Drama is a literary work written mainly to be performed by actors.

  • Dialogue: Words spoken by characters.
  • Stage Directions: Instructions about movements and actions.
  • Acts: Major divisions of a play.
  • Scenes: Smaller divisions within an act.

Types of Drama

  • Tragedy: A serious play often involving suffering or an unhappy outcome.
  • Comedy: A play that uses humor and often ends happily.
  • Tragicomedy: Combines serious and humorous elements.
  • Melodrama: Emphasizes strong emotions and clear conflicts.

What is Prose?

Prose is ordinary written or spoken language arranged in sentences and paragraphs.

  • Fiction: Imaginative or invented stories.
  • Nonfiction: Writing based on real people, events, or information.
REMEMBER: Drama = Written for performance. Prose = Ordinary language.
10. Multimodal Text Production

What is Multimodal Text?

A multimodal text communicates through two or more modes of communication.

Five Modes

  • Linguistic: Written or spoken words.
  • Visual: Images, colors, and graphics.
  • Audio: Music, sound effects, and spoken sounds.
  • Gestural: Body movements and facial expressions.
  • Spatial: Arrangement and positioning of elements.

Examples

  • Movies and short films.
  • Infographics.
  • Presentations.
  • Advertisements.
  • Digital stories.

Three Stages of Film Production

  1. Pre-production: Planning the story, script, characters, and locations.
  2. Production: Recording or filming scenes.
  3. Post-production: Editing the video and adding sound or effects.
REMEMBER: Multimodal = Combining different modes to communicate a message.
